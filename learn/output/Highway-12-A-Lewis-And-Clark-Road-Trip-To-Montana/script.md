## Narration

Before there was a highway, before there was a map, this land was home. For thousands of years the mountains and rivers of the Northwest belonged to nations who had always been here — among them the Nez Perce, the Salish, the Shoshone, and the Blackfeet. As the Nez Perce put it today: "We did not travel here; we are of this land. We did not declare our independence; we have always been free." Hold on to that, because the road we're about to drive is stunningly beautiful — and its story is more complicated than the names on the map suggest.

In 1803, President Thomas Jefferson bought the vast Louisiana Territory from France — land France did not truly own, and that was already home to dozens of Native peoples. Then he sent Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, and a party of soldiers they called the Corps of Discovery, to chart a route to the Pacific. It was a genuine feat of endurance and courage. It was also a military reconnaissance — the opening move in claiming a continent that was never empty.

They could not have done it alone. The expedition's most famous member is a young woman named Sacagawea, and the story is usually told as romance. The facts are harder. She was Lemhi Shoshone, taken captive around the age of twelve in a raid, and married while still a girl to a French-Canadian fur trader decades older. She was just sixteen or seventeen, carrying a two-month-old son, when she crossed the continent — digging roots, interpreting, and, when the Corps reached her own Shoshone people, helping win the horses that got them over the Rockies. Her descendants' nations say it plainly: history got her story wrong. On a trip like this, it's worth getting it right.

Now, the drive. It begins eastbound on Interstate ninety, climbing out of Washington's Cascades onto the wide, sunlit shrub-steppe. At Vantage, the road meets the Columbia — a river at the center of Plateau life for thousands of years before it ever carried the Corps toward the sea. Stop at the Ginkgo Petrified Forest, where stone logs fifteen million years old lie beside the water, and then turn south.

Follow the river down to two towns that face each other across the current: Clarkston, Washington, and Lewiston, Idaho — named, of course, for the explorers. But this is the heart of the Nimíipuu, the Nez Perce homeland, where the Snake meets the Clearwater at the mouth of Hells Canyon, the deepest gorge in North America. The names honor two visitors. The land belongs to a people who were here long before them — and who are here still.

From here, U.S. Highway twelve, the Lewis and Clark Highway, climbs into the mountains along the Clearwater and then the Lochsa — "rough water," in the Nez Perce language. It's one of the wildest drives left in America: nearly a hundred roadless miles of forest, whitewater, and hot springs steaming right at the edge of the pavement. And it follows the expedition's hardest chapter. In September of 1805, crossing the Bitterroots on the ancient Lolo Trail, the Corps hit early snow, lost the path, and nearly starved — eating candle wax and their own horses. When they finally staggered down to the Weippe Prairie, it was the Nez Perce who chose to feed them rather than let them die, and who helped them carve the canoes that carried them on toward the ocean.

Hold that mercy in mind at the summit of Lolo Pass — because the story does not end there. Seventy-two years later, in 1877, after gold and settlers had taken their land and the treaties had been broken, the non-treaty Nez Perce were driven from that same homeland. More than eight hundred people, with two thousand horses, fled over this very pass into Montana, believing the army would not follow them. It did. At the Big Hole, just south of here, soldiers attacked a sleeping village before dawn; between sixty and ninety Nez Perce were killed, most of them women and children. The people who once saved the Corps of Discovery were answered with a war, and a flight of more than a thousand miles. Drive Lolo Pass knowing that both of those crossings happened right here.

Just over the pass lies a quiet meadow, Traveler's Rest, near Lolo, Montana — one of the only sites where hard archaeological evidence of the expedition has ever been found. Lewis and Clark camped here going west and coming home. A short run beyond it is Missoula, a river town ringed by mountains, where the valley finally opens wide.

Turn north, and you enter the Flathead Valley — and the Flathead Indian Reservation, home today to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes. Flathead Lake shines beneath the Mission Mountains: the largest natural freshwater lake in the West, clear enough to see thirty feet down. Beyond it waits the finale — Glacier National Park, whose eastern peaks rise along the Blackfeet Nation, on land the Blackfeet gave up under hard pressure. Drive the Going to the Sun Road as it climbs to Logan Pass, past waterfalls and the last of the glaciers, and let yourself see that this beauty has both an owner and a history.

So there's the route: from the Columbia at Vantage, through Nez Perce country and over Lolo Pass, to Missoula, the Flathead, and Glacier. You'll have driven the length of the Lewis and Clark Highway — and, if you let it, come away with a fuller story than the road signs tell. A story of real courage and endurance. And of a homeland that was taken, defended, and never surrendered.

So go and drive it. Fill the tank in Kooskia — there really is nothing for the next hundred miles. Bring your curiosity, and bring your respect. The land is breathtaking. It is also, still, someone's home. [[pause:400ms]] Travel it that way.
